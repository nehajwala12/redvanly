How much should Redvanly spend on marketing? Less on paid, placed differently, and tested before the next step up.

A preview of the diagnostic CreditSwan would run on Redvanly's $19M ecommerce business. It covers the budget question first, then the channel, funnel, email and measurement issues that decide it. Everything here is scoped to ecommerce, not the $50M total company.

How to read this preview

Each figure carries a basis tag. Call figures come from our conversation with the Redvanly team. Public figures come from the storefront, published policies and industry sources. Model figures are synthetic, calibrated to the call figures so the whole picture adds up. In a live engagement every Model figure is replaced by Redvanly's own Shopify, analytics, ad-platform and email data in the first week. The conclusions show the method and the likely shape of the answer. They are not yet findings.

Six conclusions

Each one links to the section that shows the working.

01 · Return on paid

Paid media returns about $1.08 of lifetime contribution per dollar. At the margin, five of six channels return less than $1.

Attributed revenue makes paid look comfortable: a 45% ratio against a 62% breakeven. Incremental revenue is roughly half of what platforms and analytics credit. Once future repeat orders are counted, the average dollar still clears breakeven. The last dollar in most channels does not.

02 · The budget answer

A $4.0M paid budget, reallocated, does better than today's $4.6M in every incrementality case we tested.

At $4.0M, revenue holds within 1% of today. In-year contribution rises by about $0.5M and contribution margin moves from 30% to 33%. The profit-maximising budget could be anywhere from $1.5M to $6.4M, depending on how incremental paid really is. That range is too wide to act on, so the next step is a holdout test, not a bigger cut.

03 · Why efficiency got harder

Efficiency slipped this year because new customers cost more. The site did not get worse.

Meta CPMs rose an estimated 13–20% year over year across the market Public. In the model, new-visitor conversion falls from 1.12% to 0.95% while returning-visitor conversion holds. That pattern points to acquisition quality and landing experience more than the store itself.

04 · The direct-traffic thesis

Direct traffic can replace some paid spend: brand search and retargeting. It cannot replace prospecting.

About a third of "direct" revenue is likely B2B and omnichannel account orders. Some of the rest is demand that prospecting creates. In the February spend cut, direct and brand-search sessions fell 9% with a one-week lag Model. A page-level A/B test cannot answer this question. A geographic holdout can.

05 · Funnel

The funnel leaks most at mobile cart-to-checkout, where delivery and return terms first become concrete.

Mobile carries 71% of sessions but converts at about a third of the desktop rate. The steepest gap is cart to checkout: 48% on mobile against 61% on desktop Model. The product page promises free 30-day returns while the policy page charges a $9 return fee Public.

06 · Owned channels

Email and SMS drive about 11% of ecommerce revenue. Comparable brands get 20–30%.

Email revenue fell about 15% year over year Model, while the benchmark for DTC brands is 20–30% of revenue Public. Moving owned channels to 14% is worth about $0.6M in revenue at almost no media cost. That is the cheapest way to replace revenue given up by cutting paid.

Marketing in total is $6.08M, or 32% of ecommerce revenue. Variable costs other than product are 18%, so each incremental revenue dollar keeps 62 cents before marketing. That 62% is the number every budget decision in this report turns on.

What the 45% ratio hides

We read the paid efficiency ratio as paid media spend divided by paid-attributed revenue: $4.6M against $10.2M, or about 2.2× attributed ROAS. With 62 cents kept per revenue dollar, attributed breakeven is a 62% ratio. On that view, 45% looks comfortable.

Attribution credits paid with roughly twice the revenue it actually causes. The model puts incremental revenue from paid at about $5.0M, a 91% ratio. That is well past breakeven on first orders. Paid pays back only when the repeat orders of the customers it acquires are counted.

If Redvanly defines the ratio differently, for example against new-customer revenue only, the levels move but the gap between attributed and incremental does not. Model

Blended, Redvanly pays back its acquisition cost on the first order. Counted only on customers paid media truly adds, the cost is $336 for a customer worth $273 over a lifetime. Paid clears breakeven overall only because it also nudges repeat orders from existing customers. That is exactly the revenue direct traffic might replace. This is why the budget question and the direct-traffic thesis are the same question.

Why is reported AOV higher than consumer AOV?
B2B and omnichannel accounts, such as clubs, corporate buyers and partner programmes, place some orders through the web store. In the model they are 1.6% of orders at about $1,150 each, $1.6M of revenue. They lift reported AOV from $205 to $220 and land mostly in "direct". The call flagged that attribution for these accounts is hard. The first fix is to tag them at checkout so consumer metrics are not distorted.
Is $440 LTV revenue or margin, and over what horizon?
We have treated $440 as lifetime revenue per customer, about two orders, and applied the 62% variable margin to get $273. If it is already a margin figure, or a 12-month figure, the planner's "Value of a new customer" control lets you see how the budget answer changes. The engagement would rebuild LTV from order-level cohorts with equal observation windows.
Why use contribution rather than ROAS to set the budget?
ROAS ignores returns, variable costs and whether the sale would have happened anyway. A budget set on contribution from the marginal dollar stops spending where the next dollar stops paying for itself. That rule works at any budget level and in any season.

How the planner works

Each channel has a diminishing-returns curve. Incremental revenue equals today's incremental revenue × (spend ÷ today's spend)b. Today's incremental revenue is attributed revenue × an incrementality factor, and b sets how quickly returns fall as spend rises. The revenue that does not depend on paid, from direct, organic, email, wholesale halo and existing customers, is held as a base. Contribution applies the gross margin and variable-cost rates, then subtracts paid media and $1.48M of other marketing. Lifetime contribution adds the future repeat orders of new customers acquired by paid.

The frontier fills each budget level channel by channel, $10K at a time, always putting the next increment where the next dollar earns most. Channel floors and caps apply: brand search keeps a defensive floor, and CTV is capped at $0.75M until a test supports more.

Channel parameters used
What the planner cannot tell you
It cannot tell you the true incrementality of each channel. That is the largest uncertainty, and the reason the "How incremental is paid?" control exists. It also ignores seasonality within the year, creative fatigue, inventory limits and the effect of ecommerce spend on wholesale and store sales. The engagement calibrates every curve on Redvanly's weekly spend and order history and then on test results.
Section 04 · Channel economics

Where the paid dollar earns, and where it only collects credit

The same $4.6M looks very different on three bases: what platforms claim, what the trusted analytics source attributes, and what is incremental. Budget should follow the last of these, measured at the margin.

Channel scorecard

Marginal return is lifetime contribution from the next $1 at today's spend. Scale means $0.95 or more. Hold means $0.75–0.95. Cut means below $0.75. The last column is the recommended $4.0M plan. All figures are Model and calibrated to the 45% ratio.

Response curves

Dots mark today's spend. Where a curve is flat at the dot, extra spend buys little. Brand search and retargeting are flat because most of the customers they reach were already on their way to buy.

What moves in the $4.0M plan

About $1.2M comes out of retargeting, brand search, Shopping and prospecting. About $0.6M goes back into non-brand search and CTV. The other $0.6M is the saving.

Channel notes

Meta prospecting: trim slightly and refocus on bottoms
The marginal return is about $0.90, just under breakeven. Prospecting is also the channel most likely to create direct and brand-search demand. We would hold it near $1.55M and point creative at the pull-on demonstration. Bottoms-first customers repeat at the highest rate (Section 07). The January holdout decides whether to scale it.
Meta retargeting: cut to a floor
Attributed ROAS is strong at 3.2×, but only about a quarter of it is incremental. These visitors have already browsed and many would return by email or direct. A Meta conversion-lift study run alongside the floor confirms the cut quickly.
Google brand search: defend, don't fund
At 4.5× attributed ROAS this looks like the best channel. It is the least incremental: people searching "Redvanly" mostly click the organic listing if no ad appears. Keep a floor where competitors bid on the brand name, and pause it in test regions in October to measure the true loss.
Google non-brand search: scale
Searches for pull-on golf pants, golf trousers and performance shorts carry real intent, and most buyers are new. This is the one large channel where more spend holds up at the margin. Expansion should follow the query map in the public research.
Shopping and Performance Max: cut back and exclude brand
Performance Max tends to collect branded and returning demand unless brand exclusions are set. Trim it and apply the exclusions, then re-read it. Whatever it still earns is closer to its real contribution.
CTV and YouTube: scale as a test, capped
Attribution tools undercount it because it rarely gets the last click. The model gives it credit for halo effects, which is why its marginal return is highest. That credit is also the least certain number in the model. Scale to at most $0.75M, bought by region so the January geo holdout can read its effect.
Section 05 · The direct-traffic thesis

Can direct traffic replace paid traffic?

The team's thesis is that a well-known brand should get more of its traffic directly and need less paid media. It is partly right. The answer depends on which paid dollars you mean, and it can be tested properly.

What "direct" actually contains

A third of direct is B2B and omnichannel account orders. They are real revenue, but paid media has nothing to do with them. Growing direct would not replace paid there. The consumer part of direct is mostly existing customers coming back, plus new customers who first met the brand somewhere else: a stockist, a partner club, a creator or an ad.

Omnichannel attribution is hard, as the team noted on the call. The practical fix is to tag account orders at checkout and to ask new customers one post-purchase question: "Where did you first hear about Redvanly?"

The February spend cut, read as a natural experiment

A 40% cut to prospecting for three weeks was followed, about a week later, by a 9% dip in direct and brand-search sessions. The dip recovered when spend came back. That implies roughly 35 cents of direct and brand-search revenue per dollar of prospecting. The platforms credit none of it. A before-and-after read like this is suggestive, not proof, because seasonality and promotions overlap.

How to test it: geography, not a page A/B test

A standard A/B test splits visitors on the site. Someone who types redvanly.com has already been exposed to whatever brought them. There is nothing left to randomise at that point. The unit that can be randomised is the market: turn spend off in some regions, keep it on in matched regions, and compare total ecommerce sales in each.

New visitors carry the decline

Returning visitors convert almost exactly as before. New visitors convert 15% less, and most new visitors now arrive from paid social. When the store keeps its returning shoppers but converts fewer strangers, the likely causes are who the ads reach and where they land. Site-wide problems would hit both groups.
